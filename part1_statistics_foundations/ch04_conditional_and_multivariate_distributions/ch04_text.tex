\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Most interesting questions are about several quantities at once, and about
what one tells you regarding another. This chapter sets up joint
distributions, pulls out the marginal and conditional distributions that
live inside them, and defines independence. Two identities --- the law of
total expectation and the law of total variance --- let you compute a mean
or a variance by conditioning, and the bivariate and multivariate normal
give the formula for the conditional that reappears later as linear
regression and as the Gaussian-process posterior.
\end{abstract}

\section{Joint, marginal, and conditional}
% ======================================================================

For two variables $X$ and $Y$ the \keyterm{joint} distribution is a mass
function $f(x,y) = P(X=x, Y=y)$ (discrete) or a density $f(x,y)$ with
$P\big((X,Y)\in A\big) = \iint_A f$ (continuous). Everything about the pair
is in $f$; the single-variable distributions are recovered by summing or
integrating the other variable away.

\begin{definition}[Marginal and conditional]
The \keyterm{marginal} of $X$ is
$f_X(x) = \sum_y f(x,y)$ (or $\int f(x,y)\,dy$). The \keyterm{conditional} of
$Y$ given $X=x$, for $f_X(x) > 0$, is
\[
  f_{Y\mid X}(y \mid x) = \frac{f(x,y)}{f_X(x)}.
\]
\end{definition}

The conditional is itself a distribution in $y$ (it is non-negative and sums
to $1$), so it has a mean of its own, the \keyterm{conditional expectation}
$E[Y \mid X = x] = \sum_y y\, f_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x)$. $X$ and $Y$ are
\keyterm{independent} when the joint factors, $f(x,y) = f_X(x)\,f_Y(y)$,
equivalently when $f_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x) = f_Y(y)$ --- knowing $X$ tells you
nothing about $Y$.

Everything in this section is mechanical once you have the joint table, which
makes it a good thing to compute rather than memorise:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
joint = {(0, 0): 0.3, (0, 1): 0.2, (1, 0): 0.1, (1, 1): 0.4}   # P(X=x, Y=y)

def marginal_x(j):
    out = {}
    for (x, y), p in j.items():
        out[x] = out.get(x, 0.0) + p
    return out

def conditional_y_given_x(j, x0):
    px = sum(p for (x, y), p in j.items() if x == x0)
    return {y: p / px for (x, y), p in j.items() if x == x0}

print(marginal_x(joint))                # {0: 0.5, 1: 0.5}
print(conditional_y_given_x(joint, 0))  # {0: 0.6, 1: 0.4}
print(conditional_y_given_x(joint, 1))  # {0: 0.2, 1: 0.8}  -- X shifts Y
\end{lstlisting}

\noindent Because $P(Y{=}1\mid X)$ moves from $0.4$ to $0.8$ as $X$ goes
from $0$ to $1$, these two are \emph{not} independent --- $X$ carries real
information about $Y$.

\section{Computing by conditioning}
% ======================================================================

Conditioning is not just descriptive; it is a tool for computing means and
variances of a single variable by splitting on another. Both laws below are
used constantly, from mixture models to the bias--variance decomposition.

\begin{theorem}[Law of total expectation]
$E[X] = E\big[E[X \mid Y]\big]$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Write the inner expectation out and swap the order of summation:
\begin{align*}
  E\big[E[X\mid Y]\big]
    &= \sum_y E[X\mid Y=y]\,f_Y(y)
     = \sum_y \sum_x x\, \frac{f(x,y)}{f_Y(y)}\, f_Y(y) \\
    &= \sum_x x \sum_y f(x,y)
     = \sum_x x\, f_X(x) = E[X].
\end{align*}
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Law of total variance]
$\operatorname{Var}(X) = E\big[\operatorname{Var}(X\mid Y)\big]
  + \operatorname{Var}\big(E[X\mid Y]\big)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $m(Y) = E[X\mid Y]$. By definition
$\operatorname{Var}(X\mid Y) = E[X^2\mid Y] - m(Y)^2$, so
$E[\operatorname{Var}(X\mid Y)] = E[X^2] - E[m(Y)^2]$ by the law of total
expectation. Also
$\operatorname{Var}(m(Y)) = E[m(Y)^2] - (E[m(Y)])^2 = E[m(Y)^2] - (E[X])^2$.
Adding the two lines, the $E[m(Y)^2]$ terms cancel and
$E[X^2] - (E[X])^2 = \operatorname{Var}(X)$ remains.
\end{proof}

\noindent In words: total variance is the average of the within-group
variances plus the variance of the group means --- ``unexplained'' plus
``explained'' spread. That split is exactly the bias--variance decomposition
in disguise.

\section{The bivariate and multivariate normal}
% ======================================================================

For jointly normal variables the conditional distribution has a clean closed
form, and it is linear in the conditioning value.

\begin{theorem}[Bivariate normal conditional]
If $(X,Y)$ is bivariate normal with means $\mu_X,\mu_Y$, standard deviations
$\sigma_X,\sigma_Y$ and correlation $\rho$, then $Y \mid X=x$ is normal with
\[
  E[Y\mid X=x] = \mu_Y + \rho\,\frac{\sigma_Y}{\sigma_X}\,(x - \mu_X),
  \qquad
  \operatorname{Var}(Y\mid X=x) = \sigma_Y^2\,(1 - \rho^2).
\]
\end{theorem}

The conditional mean is a straight line in $x$ --- this is where linear
regression comes from --- and the conditional variance is \emph{smaller}
than the marginal $\sigma_Y^2$ by the factor $1-\rho^2$: conditioning on a
correlated variable removes a share of the uncertainty. The figure shows a
sample with $\rho = 0.7$; the slice of $Y$ at $X \approx 1$ matches the
normal the formula predicts.

\end{figure}

The same statement in full generality uses vectors and matrices. Partition a
multivariate normal vector as $(X_1, X_2)$ with mean $(\mu_1,\mu_2)$ and
covariance blocks $\Sigma_{11},\Sigma_{12},\Sigma_{21},\Sigma_{22}$. Then
$X_1 \mid X_2 = x_2$ is normal with
\[
  \mu_{1\mid 2} = \mu_1 + \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}(x_2 - \mu_2),
  \qquad
  \Sigma_{1\mid 2} = \Sigma_{11} - \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{21}.
\]
This single formula is the engine of Gaussian-process regression in Part III:
predicting at new inputs is conditioning a big multivariate normal on the
observed outputs. A warning worth keeping: zero correlation does \emph{not}
mean independence in general --- but for variables that are \emph{jointly}
normal, uncorrelated does imply independent, because $\rho = 0$ collapses the
conditional mean to $\mu_Y$ and the joint density factors.

\begin{summarybox}
A joint distribution $f(x,y)$ contains the marginals $f_X,f_Y$ (sum the other
variable away) and the conditionals $f_{Y\mid X} = f(x,y)/f_X(x)$;
independence is $f = f_X f_Y$. The law of total expectation
($E[X]=E[E[X\mid Y]]$) and the law of total variance
($\operatorname{Var}(X)=E[\operatorname{Var}(X\mid Y)]+\operatorname{Var}(E[X\mid Y])$)
compute by conditioning. For the normal, $X_1\mid X_2$ is again normal with a
linear mean $\mu_1+\Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}(x_2-\mu_2)$ and reduced
covariance $\Sigma_{11}-\Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{21}$.
\end{summarybox}
