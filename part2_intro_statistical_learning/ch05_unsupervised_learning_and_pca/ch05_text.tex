\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Principal component analysis finds the few directions along which data vary
the most, and throws the rest away. It is the workhorse of dimensionality
reduction, and it has a striking double justification: the directions that
\emph{capture the most variance} are exactly the ones that \emph{lose the
least} when you reconstruct the data from them --- and both are the top
eigenvectors of the covariance matrix. This chapter sets up the projection,
proves that equivalence, and connects it to the singular value decomposition
that computes it in practice.
\end{abstract}

\section{Directions of greatest variance}
% ======================================================================

Centre the data so each feature has mean zero, and collect it in an
$n\times d$ matrix $X$ with covariance $\Sigma = \tfrac1n X^\top X$. Project a
point onto a unit direction $w$ and you get the scalar $w^\top x$; the
variance of those projections across the data is
\[
  \operatorname{Var}(w^\top x) = w^\top \Sigma\, w.
\]
The \keyterm{first principal component} is the unit direction that makes this
as large as possible; the second is the best direction orthogonal to it, and
so on. Projecting onto the top $k$ components keeps the most variance any
$k$ directions could.

In practice you do not search directions by hand --- the top component is the
dominant eigenvector of $\Sigma$, which a few matrix-vector products find by
\keyterm{power iteration}:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
def mat_vec(M, v):
    return [sum(M[i][j]*v[j] for j in range(len(v))) for i in range(len(M))]

def normalize(v):
    s = sum(c*c for c in v) ** 0.5
    return [c/s for c in v]

def power_iteration(M, iters=300):          # top eigenvector of symmetric M
    v = normalize([1.0] + [0.0]*(len(M)-1))
    for _ in range(iters):
        v = normalize(mat_vec(M, v))
    lam = sum(vi*mi for vi, mi in zip(v, mat_vec(M, v)))   # Rayleigh quotient
    return lam, v

cov = [[2.0, 0.8], [0.8, 0.6]]
lam, pc1 = power_iteration(cov)
print(round(lam, 3))                        # 2.363  (largest eigenvalue)
print([round(c, 3) for c in pc1])           # [0.911, 0.413]  (PC1 direction)
\end{lstlisting}

\noindent The eigenvalue is the variance captured along that component. The
figure shows the two principal directions of a correlated cloud and the
projection onto the first --- a single number per point that still holds most
of the spread.

\section{One answer to two questions}
% ======================================================================

PCA can be posed two ways, and they give the same components.

\begin{theorem}[Variance maximisation]
The unit vector $w$ maximising $w^\top\Sigma w$ is the eigenvector of
$\Sigma$ with the largest eigenvalue, and the maximum value is that
eigenvalue.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Maximise $w^\top\Sigma w$ subject to $w^\top w = 1$. The Lagrangian is
$w^\top\Sigma w - \lambda(w^\top w - 1)$; setting its gradient to zero gives
\[
  \Sigma w = \lambda w,
\]
so $w$ is an eigenvector and $\lambda$ its eigenvalue. At such a $w$ the
objective is $w^\top\Sigma w = \lambda$, so the maximum is attained at the
\emph{largest} eigenvalue. The spectral theorem guarantees $\Sigma$ (symmetric,
positive semidefinite) has an orthonormal eigenbasis, which gives the
remaining components in order.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Reconstruction-error minimisation]
Among all rank-$k$ orthogonal projections $P = WW^\top$, the one minimising
the reconstruction error $\sum_i \|x_i - P x_i\|^2$ is the projection onto the
top $k$ eigenvectors of $\Sigma$ --- the same components.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[Sketch]
By the Pythagorean theorem $\|x\|^2 = \|Px\|^2 + \|x - Px\|^2$, so minimising
the reconstruction error $\sum_i\|x_i - Px_i\|^2$ is the same as maximising
the retained energy $\sum_i\|Px_i\|^2 = \operatorname{tr}(W^\top \Sigma W)$.
That trace is maximised, over orthonormal $W$, by the top $k$ eigenvectors
(a direct consequence of the first theorem applied one direction at a time).
\end{proof}

\noindent So ``keep the most variance'' and ``lose the least information'' are
not two heuristics that happen to agree --- they are the same optimisation,
and its solution is the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix.

\section{Singular values, how many components, and the fine print}
% ======================================================================

In practice PCA is computed from the \keyterm{singular value decomposition}
$X = U D V^\top$, not by forming $\Sigma$. The right singular vectors
(columns of $V$) are the principal components, and the singular values relate
to the eigenvalues of $\Sigma$ by $\sigma_j^2 = n\lambda_j$. Working from $X$
directly is more numerically stable than squaring it into $X^\top X$.

How many components to keep is read from the eigenvalues: the fraction of
variance explained by component $j$ is $\lambda_j / \sum_\ell \lambda_\ell$,
and you keep enough that the cumulative fraction crosses a threshold --- the
``elbow'' of the \keyterm{scree plot}. The error you accept is exactly the
variance you drop, $\sum_{j>k}\lambda_j$.

Two cautions keep PCA honest. It is \emph{linear}: it finds flat subspaces, so
data lying on a curved manifold needs \keyterm{kernel PCA} or a nonlinear
method. And it equates variance with importance, so features must be put on a
common scale first (standardise, or PCA just tracks whichever feature has the
biggest units). Within those limits, PCA is the first thing to reach for when
there are more dimensions than you can look at.

\begin{summarybox}
PCA projects onto the directions of greatest variance, which are the top
eigenvectors of the covariance $\Sigma$ --- found by power iteration or, in
practice, the SVD. The components maximise retained variance
$w^\top\Sigma w$ and, equivalently, minimise reconstruction error; both reduce
to $\Sigma w = \lambda w$, so the two views give one answer. The variance
explained by component $j$ is $\lambda_j/\sum_\ell\lambda_\ell$, which is how
you choose how many to keep. PCA is linear and scale-sensitive, so standardise
first.
\end{summarybox}
